framework: cloud-based data platforms and the Data Lake paradigm, Big Data processing 
on ARM hardware, and Infrastructure as Code for cluster provisioning. For each topic, 
existing results are summarised and open gaps identified.

Cloud computing extends the distributed-systems paradigm into an on-demand service 
model. In the Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS) layer, providers supply compute, 
storage, and networking resources that consumers assemble into custom application 
stacks \citep{tanenbaum2017distributed}. For data-intensive workloads, this model has 
given rise to two dominant storage paradigms. The \emph{Data Warehouse} enforces a 
\emph{schema-on-write} discipline, requiring data to be cleaned, transformed, and 
modelled before ingestion --- reliable, but rigid and costly to extend. The 
\emph{Data Lake}, in contrast, stores raw data under a \emph{schema-on-read} model, 
offering ingestion flexibility at the risk of poor governance if cataloguing is 
neglected.

Hybrid approaches have attracted growing attention. \citet{nuthalapati2024architecting} and \citet{sundar2022comprehensive} 
propose cloud-based \emph{lakehouse} architectures that combine scalable raw storage 
with structured governance layers, arguing that systematic organisation is necessary 
to prevent \emph{data-swamp} scenarios. In the public sector, however, adoption faces 
barriers beyond technical design. Through a systematic literature review, \citet{didas} finds that many Big Data analytics projects in public institutions fall 
short of expectations, largely because of high capital investment requirements and 
limited organisational maturity to sustain infrastructure, data quality, and security 
throughout the lifecycle. These barriers motivate the search for entry-level platforms 
that lower the financial threshold without forfeiting the core distributed-storage and 
processing capabilities that analytical workloads require.

The Hadoop ecosystem addresses the distributed-processing requirement by coupling HDFS 
for block-based, replicated storage with parallel execution engines such as MapReduce 
and Spark \citep{hadoop_hdfs_design_2025, marz2015bigdata}. Clemlab's OpenSource Data 
Platform (ODP) packages these components for streamlined cluster deployment, with 
Apache Ambari providing centralised orchestration and monitoring 
\citep{clemlab_odp_docs_intro_1310}. Oracle Cloud Infrastructure (OCI) documents its
IaaS offerings through publicly accessible guides \citep{oci_docs}, and its perpetual
Free Tier includes ARM-based Ampere A1 instances --- an opportunity to assemble a
multi-node Hadoop cluster at zero monetary cost that remains largely unexplored in
the literature.

The financial viability of cloud deployments has been examined primarily through the
lens of cost optimisation on paid tiers. Right-sizing instances, leveraging spot
pricing, and reserving capacity are common strategies
\citep{deochake2026cloudaiinfrastructurecost}, and FinOps frameworks have formalised
the discipline of aligning cloud spending with business value
\citep{vo2025finopsagentusecase}. Industry surveys confirm that cost management
remains the top cloud challenge for the majority of organisations
\citep{flexera2025stateofcloud}. These approaches, however, assume a non-zero
expenditure baseline. The possibility of operating a full Big Data stack within a
perpetual free-tier allocation---where the cost is strictly zero but the resource
ceiling is severe---has not been systematically investigated.

Processor architectures for data-centre workloads fall broadly into two families: the 
Complex Instruction Set Computer (CISC) tradition, represented by x86-64, and the 
Reduced Instruction Set Computer (RISC) tradition, exemplified by ARM. ARM processors 
execute simpler instructions at lower energy cost per operation, yielding higher power 
efficiency and lower acquisition cost \citep{hennessy2019computer}. The Hadoop ecosystem, 
however, was historically developed for and tested on x86-64 hardware, creating 
potential compatibility barriers for ARM deployments --- particularly in the 
availability of pre-compiled packages and JVM optimisations \citep{8287751}.

Two empirical studies are directly relevant. \citet{loghin2015performance} benchmarked
Big Data workloads on clusters of small nodes, including ARM-based boards, and showed
that I/O-bound workloads are less sensitive to processor micro-architecture than
CPU-bound ones, making low-power nodes a viable option when throughput per core is not
the primary constraint. \citet{8287751} evaluated Hadoop and Spark on commodity ARM64
servers and reported favourable cost-efficiency ratios, concluding that ARM-based
clusters represent a promising direction for budget-sensitive deployments. Their
experiments, however, were conducted on hardware with considerably higher per-node
resources than those available on a cloud free tier. Both studies relied on manually
provisioned, bare-metal or dedicated clusters and did not incorporate Infrastructure
as Code tooling into the deployment workflow. Neither addressed the automated,
end-to-end deployment of the Hadoop stack on ARM hardware, nor operated under the
extreme resource constraints of a cloud free tier, where each node is limited to
1~vCPU and 6~GB of RAM and the total cluster capacity does not exceed 4~vCPUs and
24~GB.

Infrastructure as Code (IaC) replaces manual, console-driven server configuration --- 
often termed \emph{ClickOps} --- with codified, version-controlled artefacts that 
define the desired state of an environment \citep{morris2016infrastructure}. Two 
properties make IaC particularly attractive for research reproducibility: 
\emph{idempotency}, whereby repeated execution converges to the same infrastructure 
state, and \emph{reproducibility}, whereby a third party can recreate the environment 
from the code alone.

\citet{Bollineni2022IaCDataEngineering} examines IaC adoption in data
engineering and argues that it mitigates configuration drift, reduces operational risk,
and improves pipeline predictability. The analysis, however, remains at the level of
general principles; it does not report on a specific, end-to-end deployment validated
through benchmarking. \citet{morris2016infrastructure} provides a comprehensive
treatment of IaC principles---idempotency, version control, and automated
testing---but focuses on general server management rather than on the particular
challenges of orchestrating a multi-service Hadoop stack. More broadly, the combination
of Terraform for declarative cloud provisioning with Ansible for procedural application
configuration is a recognised industry practice, yet its application to deploying a
full Hadoop ecosystem on free-tier ARM instances has not, to our knowledge, been
documented in the academic literature.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent
In summary, prior work has established the viability of ARM processors for Big Data
workloads, the value of IaC for infrastructure management, and the importance of cost
optimisation in cloud deployments, but these threads have not been brought together. No
published study offers a fully automated pipeline for deploying a Hadoop-based Data
Lake on free-tier ARM cloud instances, validated through a reproducible benchmark. The
present work addresses this gap by combining Terraform and Ansible into a single
pipeline that provisions and configures a four-node Hadoop cluster on the OCI Free
Tier and by evaluating the resulting environment under the Star Schema Benchmark.
